      Permanent residence and citizenship

      The usual road is: student permit, then a post-study job-search permit, then a work permit, then permanent residence, then citizenship if you want it. How long each step takes depends on the country and on whether you are an EU or a non-EU citizen.

      EU / EEAFree movement

      You need no visa and no work permit. After 5 years of continuous legal residence in another EU country you gain a permanent right of residence. Citizenship is optional and follows each country's own naturalisation rules.

      Non-EUStep by step

      You move from student permit to a job-search permit, then to a work permit or EU Blue Card, then to long-term or permanent residence, and finally to citizenship. Study years often count only partly, or not at all, towards permanent residence.

      Rules that changed recently

      Citizenship rules are being tightened in many countries. These changes were confirmed as of September 2026.

      SwedenSince 6 June 2026 the general residence requirement is 8 years (was 5), with new language, civics and income tests.
      PortugalSince 19 May 2026 naturalisation needs 10 years of legal residence (7 for EU and Portuguese-speaking-country citizens), counted from your residence permit.
      GermanyCitizenship needs 5 years and dual nationality is allowed. The 3-year fast track was abolished in October 2025.
      FranceSince 1 January 2026 naturalisation needs French at B2 and a civics exam. The 5-year period can drop to 2 years after French higher education.
      FinlandCitizenship needs 8 years (5 if you meet the language requirement) since October 2024. Permanent residence needs 6 years since January 2026, with shorter routes for higher earners and master's graduates.
      United KingdomThe Graduate Route is 24 months now and falls to 18 months for applications from 1 January 2027. Settlement is still 5 years on skilled routes, but a 10-year "earned settlement" model has been proposed and is not yet law.
      NetherlandsNaturalisation is still 5 years. A plan for 10 years has not become law.

      Six lessons before you decide

      The ranking is only as good as your priorities. These lessons explain what sits behind the scores.

      Lesson 1What a European CS bachelor's looks like

      Most of Europe follows the Bologna system. A bachelor's is 180 ECTS credits, which is normally three years. Scotland and some Irish programmes take four.

      You study computer science from day one. Year one is usually calculus, linear algebra, discrete maths, programming and algorithms, with little general education. It is fast and maths-heavy.

      Bachelor's and master's are separate applications. You can choose a country for your bachelor's based on cost and language, then move to another for a prestigious master's.

      Lesson 2Language decides more than rankings

      Bachelor's degrees are far more likely to be taught in the local language than master's degrees. In Germany, Austria, Switzerland, France, Italy, Spain and the Nordics, most CS bachelor's are taught in the local language, and you usually need about B2 to C1 to enter.

      English-taught CS bachelor's are easy to find in Ireland, the UK, the Netherlands, Malta, Denmark, Estonia, Poland, Hungary and several other Central and Eastern European countries.

      If you are willing to learn German for a year or two, Germany becomes one of the best-value options in Europe. If you are not, the list of realistic countries changes a lot. The "English only" switch in the finder shows you that list.

      Lesson 3Add up the real cost

      Tuition is only one of three costs. The other two are living costs, where rent is the biggest, and the proof of funds that many visas require. Flights, insurance and visa fees come on top.

      Where you come from changes the picture. EU/EEA students pay little or nothing in Germany and the Nordics. Non-EU students pay heavily in the Netherlands, Ireland, the UK, Denmark and Norway.

      Housing is the hardest part in Amsterdam, Dublin, Munich, Copenhagen, Zurich and Lisbon. Start looking as soon as you accept an offer. Student work is usually capped at around 16 to 20 hours a week in term time, so do not count on it to pay tuition.

      Lesson 4Reputation versus fit

      World rankings mostly measure research output. For CS in Europe, the best-known names include ETH Zurich, EPFL, Oxford, Cambridge, Imperial, UCL, TU Munich and TU Delft.

      A famous name you cannot afford, or a first-year exam you cannot pass, is not a good deal. ETH's first-year exams, for example, are known to be tough. Well-taught programmes at places like Eindhoven, Aalto, DTU, Tartu or KIT can put you in a strong position, especially with a good job market around them.

      When comparing programmes, read the curriculum, check the amount of maths in year one, and look for internships or work placements.

      Lesson 5Jobs, and staying after you graduate

      Employers mostly hire near where you study, so your city matters. Ireland hosts the European headquarters of many large tech firms. The Netherlands has Amsterdam and Eindhoven. Germany has Munich and Berlin. Switzerland has Zurich. The Nordics have Stockholm, Copenhagen and Helsinki. Estonia has a strong startup scene, and Poland, Romania and Serbia have large IT and outsourcing sectors.

      Most countries offer a post-study permit to look for work, from a few months to a few years. The table below shows the picture, but these rules change often, so always check the official immigration site.

      Lesson 6What you usually need to apply

      • Your school-leaving certificate, with strong maths (and often physics).
      • Proof of English, often IELTS around 6.0 to 6.5 or TOEFL iBT around 80 to 90, for English-taught programmes. Some universities accept other tests or your school English grade.
      • A passport, CV and usually a motivation letter. Some countries also ask for recommendation letters or entrance exams (Italy and Finland are known for them).
      • Proof that you can pay, plus health insurance, for your visa or residence permit.

      Scholarships for bachelor's degrees are rarer than for master's degrees. Look at national schemes such as Stipendium Hungaricum (Hungary) and university-level scholarships, and check whether your school qualification is accepted directly.

      Four lessons before you choose a business degree

      The ranking is only as good as your priorities. These lessons explain what sits behind the scores for commerce and business.

      Lesson 1What a European business bachelor's looks like

      A bachelor's is usually 180 ECTS credits, or three years. "Commerce" is the name used in Ireland and other English-speaking systems for business degrees. Elsewhere you will see Business Administration, Economics and Management, International Business, or Betriebswirtschaftslehre in Germany.

      Year one is normally accounting, micro and macroeconomics, statistics, maths, management and marketing. You specialise in finance, marketing, accounting or entrepreneurship in the later years, and the maths content is higher in economics and finance tracks.

      Bachelor's and master's are separate applications. A general bachelor's in a low-cost country, followed by a specialised master's at a well-known business school, is a common and sensible route.

      Lesson 2Accreditation and brand matter

      In business, the school's brand and network count for more than in most fields. Look for the international accreditations AACSB, EQUIS and AMBA. Schools with two or three of them are among the best.

      Public universities charge modest fees in many countries. Private business schools, such as the French grandes écoles, IE University, ESADE, WHU or Bocconi, charge much more but offer strong employer links. Compare what you would pay against the recruiters who visit.

      Look for degrees with an internship or a placement year. Employers value work experience alongside the degree, especially for consulting, banking and audit.

      Lesson 3Language and cost

      English-taught business bachelor's are common in the Netherlands, Denmark, Ireland, the UK, Malta, Cyprus, Poland, Hungary, the Baltics and at private schools in Spain, Italy and Portugal. Germany, France, Austria and Switzerland teach most public business degrees in the local language.

      Fees follow the same pattern as other subjects: EU/EEA students pay little in Germany, Austria, the Nordics and much of Central Europe, while non-EU students pay heavily in the UK, Ireland, the Netherlands and Denmark. Poland, Hungary, Czechia, Romania and the Baltics give the best value for English-taught business degrees.

      Lesson 4Jobs, and staying after you graduate

      Business jobs cluster in financial and corporate hubs: London, Frankfurt, Zurich, Paris, Amsterdam, Dublin, Luxembourg, Stockholm and Milan. Big Four firms, banks, consultancies and shared-service centres also hire in Warsaw, Bucharest, Prague, Budapest and Vilnius.

      Strong quantitative skills (statistics, Excel, Python or SQL) give you an edge. Professional qualifications such as ACCA, CFA and CPA can be studied alongside your degree. Most countries offer a post-study job-search permit, but rules change often, so check the official immigration site.

      Four lessons before you choose medicine or health

      Medicine is more regulated than any other field. These lessons explain what to check before you spend years and a lot of money on it.

      Lesson 1Medicine is not a normal bachelor's

      In most of Europe, medicine, dentistry, pharmacy and veterinary medicine are single-cycle degrees of 5 to 6 years (300 to 360 ECTS). You do not do a bachelor's first. Nursing, midwifery, physiotherapy and biomedical sciences are usually 3 to 4 year bachelor's degrees.

      In this guide, "Bachelor's" means these undergraduate and single-cycle degrees, and "Master's" means specialised MSc degrees such as biomedical sciences, public health or bioinformatics. Doctors' specialty training comes after the degree.

      Admission is by test and competition, not just grades. Check the test, its registration date and the number of places for international applicants a year before you plan to start.

      Lesson 2Check recognition and your licence to practise

      Doctors, dentists, nurses, pharmacists and midwives trained in an EU country have their degree recognised across the EU under EU rules. A degree from outside the EU, such as Serbia or the UK after Brexit, is not recognised automatically.

      To work as a doctor you also need a licence from the national medical authority, which usually needs a language exam and sometimes a licensing exam. If you plan to practise in your home country, check its recognition rules and any licensing exam for foreign graduates before you apply.

      Lesson 3Language and admissions tests

      Most public medical degrees in Germany, Austria, France, Spain, the Nordics, the Netherlands and Switzerland are taught in the local language and are very competitive. English-taught medicine is available in Ireland, the UK, Italy (through the IMAT), Poland, Czechia, Hungary, Slovakia, Romania, Bulgaria, Croatia, Lithuania, Latvia and at some universities in Serbia and Cyprus.

      Typical tests are the UCAT (UK), the IMAT (Italy), the MedAT (Austria), an aptitude test in Switzerland, and biology and chemistry entrance exams in Central and Eastern Europe. Even in English-taught programmes you will need the local language for clinical placements with patients.

      Lesson 4Add up the real cost

      Public Italian medicine in English is among the cheapest, with income-based fees, but places are few and the test is very competitive. Romania, Bulgaria and Serbia cost roughly €5,000 to €9,000 a year. Poland, Czechia, Hungary, Slovakia, the Baltics and Croatia usually cost roughly €9,000 to €18,000 a year. The UK and Ireland cost far more, often €45,000 or more a year.

      Medicine takes 6 years, so multiply the yearly cost by six and add living costs. Nursing, pharmacy or biomedical sciences are cheaper and shorter, and a good route to a health career.

      A realistic application calendar

      For an autumn start. Deadlines differ by country and university, so treat these as a guide.

      Medicine starts earlier. Admissions tests are often booked a year ahead (for example UCAT registration opens in the spring, the UCAS deadline for medicine is in mid-October, the IMAT is held in September and Dutch medicine closes on 15 January). Start your plan 18 to 24 months before you want to begin.
      1. 18 to 12 months before

        Use the finder to shortlist three to five countries. Check whether your school qualification is accepted directly, since Germany, for example, may ask some applicants to complete a foundation year first. Start preparing for language tests (IELTS, TOEFL, or a German, French or other language test).

      2. 12 to 9 months before

        Pick specific programmes. Read each curriculum, entry requirements and fee page. Write every deadline in one place.

      3. 9 to 5 months before

        Apply. Rough guides: Sweden and Finland around January, the Netherlands by 1 May (earlier for selective programmes), UK universities from late January, Denmark mid-March, and Germany mid-July for the winter semester, sometimes earlier through central application services. Always confirm on the university page.

      4. 5 to 2 months before

        Accept an offer and apply for your visa or residence permit. Prepare proof of funds, such as a blocked bank account in Germany.

      5. 2 months before, until arrival

        Book housing, health insurance and travel. Arrive early. Orientation weeks are where you meet your first friends.

      Common questions

      Short answers to what students ask most.

      What is the difference between EU and non-EU students?

      EU/EEA citizens have free movement: no student visa, no work permit and, in many countries, home-student tuition fees. Non-EU citizens need a student visa or residence permit, must prove they can pay, often pay higher tuition, and have work limits during study. Switch the "EU / EEA" and "Non-EU" buttons anywhere on this page to see the difference for your case.

      Which country is cheapest for a CS degree?

      It depends on where you are from, your level and your language. For non-EU students, public universities in Germany (where most are free), plus Poland, Czechia, Romania, Bulgaria, Serbia and the Baltic states, are usually the cheapest overall. For EU/EEA students, Germany, the Nordics, Austria and much of Central Europe are free or very cheap. Use the budget slider in the finder to see it for your profile.

      Which country is cheapest for a business degree?

      It depends on where you are from, your level and your language. Public universities in Germany and Austria are free or cheap but mostly teach business in the local language. For English-taught business degrees, Poland, Hungary, Czechia, Romania, Bulgaria and the Baltic states are usually the best value for non-EU students. Private business schools in France, Spain, Italy and Switzerland cost much more. Use the budget slider in the finder to see it for your profile.

      Which country is cheapest for medicine in English?

      Public universities in Italy (through the IMAT test) have the lowest fees, but places are limited and the test is competitive. Romania, Bulgaria and Serbia cost roughly €5,000 to €9,000 a year. Poland, Czechia, Hungary, Slovakia, the Baltics and Croatia cost more, roughly €9,000 to €18,000 a year. Germany and the Nordics have low or no fees, but medicine is taught in the local language and admission is very competitive.

      Will my medical degree let me work as a doctor?

      A medical degree from an EU country is recognised across the EU, but you still need a licence from the national medical authority and usually a language exam. A degree from outside the EU is not recognised automatically. If you want to practise in your home country, check its recognition rules and any licensing exam for foreign graduates before you apply.

      Does the business school's accreditation matter?

      It helps. AACSB, EQUIS and AMBA are the main international accreditations, and recruiters and master's admissions teams recognise them. Many good public universities have no accreditation and are still respected locally, so also check the curriculum, internships and graduate outcomes.

      Do I need IELTS or TOEFL?

      Most English-taught programmes ask for proof of English. IELTS around 6.0 to 6.5 for bachelor's and 6.5 for master's, or TOEFL iBT around 80 to 100, is common. Some universities accept a school English grade, a previous degree taught in English, or another test. Check each programme page.

      Can I work while studying?

      EU/EEA students can work freely. Most countries let non-EU students work part-time, often about 16 to 20 hours a week in term time and more in holidays, but the rules differ. Pay rarely covers tuition and rent, so plan your budget without relying on it. Each country guide lists its own rule.

      Do I need to learn the local language?

      Not for English-taught programmes, but a basic level helps with housing, part-time jobs and daily life. For degrees taught in the local language you must pass a language test first. For citizenship you will almost always need to pass a language test in the national language.

      How long does a student visa take?

      It ranges from a few weeks to several months, depending on the country and the season. Start the visa steps as soon as you have your admission letter, and book your appointment early because summer is the busiest time.

      Can I stay and work after I graduate?

      EU/EEA students can stay and work freely in EU countries. Non-EU students usually get a job-search permit lasting from a few months to two or three years, then need a work permit. The country guides and the table in the residence section show the details.

      How long until I can get permanent residence or citizenship?

      Permanent residence often takes about 4 to 6 years of legal residence, and citizenship about 5 to 10 years, and many countries have recently made this longer. Study years often count only partly. Language tests are usually required for both. Check the residence table and the "rules that changed recently" box.

      Is a three-year bachelor's accepted everywhere?

      Bologna Process degrees are widely recognised across Europe and beyond. If you plan to study or work in your home country afterwards, check its recognition rules for foreign degrees before you apply.

      Can I apply to more than one country?

      Yes, and most people do. Apply to a mix of ambitious, realistic and safe options, and keep every deadline in one calendar. Some countries (such as the UK and the Netherlands) use central systems with a limited number of choices, so read the rules first.
